# For principals

**Four minutes. Nothing to install, and no command line on this page.**

An **agent** — think of it as a capable new hire who reads fast, works
overnight, and is sometimes confidently wrong — screened a real TxDOT job from
public records. SH16 through Bexar County, 8.69 miles. It produced a bid
memo, a flagged parcel table with statutory notice periods, and a
[crew-day build-up](https://github.com/RickSmith/survey-recon/blob/main/project-sh16/crew-day.md)
with its arithmetic shown. A licensed surveyor directed and checked it.

*Not legal advice — a reading of public documents, so your counsel and carrier
start from something concrete.*

## What it costs

In hours, the unit you already bill. This corridor came out at **38 crew-days**
in the field — 299.29 hours, **2 people** to a crew — and **18 days in the
office**, 138.26 hours. The two are never added: they are bought from different
people.

The field figure is a **floor**. 2 lines of it have no total at all, and those
hours are missing from it rather than zero in it. Nobody has counted the
manholes and culverts that decide how often traffic control goes out. And nobody
has said how much of the retracement falls on the centerline — which is not only
more hours, it is two shadow vehicles and two operators instead of the crew's own
truck.

**An agent works the office half, not the field half.** How much faster is not
measured here, and this page will not invent a number.

Showing the arithmetic lets you **argue with one rate, not with a total**. Corner
recovery is rate `A4` — half an hour on each of 524 tracts, 262.00 hours, the
largest line. Halve it and the field number moves more than any other change.

### Rework is the argument. Speed is not.

TxDOT: *"There is no acceptable failure rate for any TxDOT survey."* A
non-compliant survey **cannot be invoiced** — hours you have paid for and cannot
bill. An agent reading every standard before the crew rolls is aimed at that.
Survey Manual (ESS), rev. April 2026, Ch. 4 —
[txdot.gov/manuals/row/ess](https://www.txdot.gov/manuals/row/ess/index.html)

## What your policy should say

Four things, on one page, signed and dated:

1. **Who signs.** Named, with a registration number
2. **What these tools are used for, and what they are not** — never work the firm
   is not already competent in
3. **What never goes into one.** Client names, unrecorded documents, live field
   data, your pricing
4. **Who reviews, and when.** Before anything leaves, not after

Mark each rule as a board rule, as board guidance, or as firm choice.
[The template does](../governance/ai-use-policy.md), so nobody can call a
preference a requirement.

## What you are still liable for

All of it. The board wrote that down on 14 November 2024, in **Policy Advisory
Opinion 71**: *"Licensees are ultimately responsible for any work product they
sign and seal."*

**22 Tex. Admin. Code § 138.33(b)** lets you seal only work done by you or under
your direct supervision — which means approving decisions **before they are acted
on** (§ 131.2(11)). An agent working overnight moves that review to the morning;
it does not remove it.
[The rules, quoted](../governance/seal-and-responsible-charge.md).

## Which one person to point at this

Somebody who writes a good scope, is fussy about sources, and has the standing to
tell a confident answer that it is wrong. Not your most technical person,
necessarily — the skill is supervision, which your firm already teaches. Hand
them [Day 0](../day-0/index.md) and half an hour.

## Who signs

**You do.** The **RPLS** whose seal goes on the work takes full professional
responsibility for it — whoever, or whatever, did the work. An agent is a tool,
like a data collector or a drafting package. Not a colleague, not a second
opinion, not a co-signer, and it cannot carry any part of the liability.
